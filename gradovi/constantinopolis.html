The City of Constantinopolis

Constantinople in Roman and Byzantine Times

Foundation as a Roman Capital

The city that became Constantinople started as the Greek settlement Byzantium. In the early 4th century AD, Emperor Constantine the Great chose it as the new capital of the Roman Empire. He rebuilt and expanded the city, naming it "New Rome," but the name that became common was Constantinople. Dedicated in 330 AD, it became a major center for administration, trade, and military defense due to its strategic location on the Bosporus strait.

Role in the Roman Empire (330–395)

As the seat of Roman power in the East, Constantinople grew rapidly. Emperors invested in infrastructure, public buildings, and fortifications. It served as the administrative center of the eastern provinces, maintaining connections with the traditional Roman institutions while developing its own identity as a political and cultural hub.

Eastern Roman and Early Byzantine Period (395–600)

After the permanent division of the Roman Empire in 395, Constantinople became the capital of the Eastern Roman Empire, later known as the Byzantine Empire. The city remained the center of imperial administration, culture, and Christian religion even as the Western Roman Empire declined. Massive walls and fortifications made it highly defensible, allowing it to resist numerous invasions.

Height of Byzantine Power (600–1025)

During the 6th century, under emperors like Justinian I, Constantinople saw major construction projects, including the Hagia Sophia. The city became a thriving center of trade, law, and Christian theology. It faced internal unrest such as the Nika riots, and external threats from Arab and Slavic forces, yet its strategic position and fortifications allowed it to remain secure and influential.

Middle Byzantine Era (1025–1204)

In the later Middle Ages, Constantinople continued as the vibrant capital of the Byzantine Empire. It was the seat of the Ecumenical Patriarchate, a religious center, and a hub of culture and politics. However, the city faced fluctuating fortunes and in 1204, during the Fourth Crusade, it was captured and controlled by the Latin Empire, suffering damage and loss of wealth. The Byzantine government continued in exile until it eventually returned, but this marked a major shift in the city’s stability and prominence.
