The City of Carthage

Carthage Under Roman Rule

The Roman Resurrection and Growth

A century after the total destruction of Punic Carthage, Julius Caesar and later Augustus founded a new city on the same site called Colonia Iulia Concordia Carthago. By the second and third centuries, Roman Carthage had grown into one of the largest and most prosperous cities in the empire. It served as the capital of the province of Africa, which was a vital source of grain for the city of Rome.

The city was famous for its grand architecture, including the massive Baths of Antoninus, which were among the largest in the Roman world. Carthage also featured a huge circus for chariot racing and a magnificent theater on Odeon Hill. Its wealth and strategic location made it a center of trade and culture in the Western Mediterranean.

Carthage as a Christian Center

Carthage became one of the most influential centers of early Christianity. It was the home of famous figures like Tertullian and Saint Cyprian. The city hosted numerous church councils that helped define Christian doctrine and the biblical canon for the Western Church. Despite periods of persecution, the Christian community flourished and built many grand basilicas throughout the city.

The Vandal Interlude and Byzantine Reconquest

In the fifth century, the Vandal tribes captured Carthage and made it the capital of their own kingdom. They ruled for about a century until the Byzantine Emperor Justinian I sent his general Belisarius to reclaim the territory. In five hundred thirty three AD, Belisarius entered the city and restored Roman authority, marking the beginning of the Byzantine period in North Africa.

Under Byzantine rule, Carthage was organized as the Exarchate of Africa. It served as a vital military bulwark against local tribes and later against the rising Arab forces. The city remained a bastion of Greco Roman culture and administration for over a hundred and sixty years, even providing the empire with a future emperor, Heraclius.

The Arab Conquest and Final Destruction

The end of Roman Carthage came in the late seventh century. Arab forces first captured the city in six hundred ninety five AD and, after a brief Byzantine recovery, they finally conquered it in six hundred ninety eight AD. To prevent any future Byzantine attempts to retake the city, the conquerors followed a scorched earth policy, destroying the walls and diverting the water supply. This marked the permanent end of Roman and Byzantine influence in the region as the center of power shifted to Tunis.
