The City of Athens

Athens During Roman Times

Roman Republic: 86 BC–27 BC

In 86 BC, Athens was captured by Roman forces led by Sulla during the First Mithridatic War. The city suffered considerable damage, including the burning of the library of the Lyceum and other cultural centers. Despite this destruction, Athens remained an important cultural and educational center. Roman presence brought political control, but the city retained much of its traditional Greek identity and continued to serve as a hub for philosophy, rhetoric, and the arts.

Roman Empire: 27 BC–395 AD

Under the Roman Empire, Athens experienced a revival and became a favored city for Roman elites seeking education and culture. The emperors Augustus, Hadrian, and others invested in public buildings, temples, theaters, and gymnasiums, enhancing the city’s infrastructure. Hadrian, in particular, contributed significantly, constructing the Library of Hadrian and the Temple of Olympian Zeus. Athens maintained its reputation as a center of philosophy and learning, attracting students and teachers from across the empire.

Eastern Roman Empire: 395–1205

After the division of the Roman Empire in 395, Athens became part of the Eastern Roman (Byzantine) Empire. The city’s political importance declined, but it remained a notable cultural and religious center. Christianity gradually replaced the old pagan traditions, and many temples were converted into churches. Athens was frequently threatened by invasions, including Slavic, Arab, and later Crusader attacks. Despite these challenges, the city preserved much of its classical heritage and continued to function as a local administrative and educational center until the arrival of the Crusaders in the early 13th century.
