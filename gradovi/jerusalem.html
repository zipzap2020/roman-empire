The City of Jerusalem

Jerusalem Under Roman and Byzantine Rule

The Roman Period and Aelia Capitolina

Roman influence began in sixty three BC when Pompey the Great captured Jerusalem. The city later saw massive expansion under Herod the Great who rebuilt the Second Temple into a magnificent structure. However, tensions between the local population and Roman authorities led to the First Jewish Revolt. This conflict ended in seventy AD when Roman legions under Titus besieged the city and destroyed the Second Temple.

Following the Bar Kokhba revolt in the second century, Emperor Hadrian decided to completely rebuild the city as a Roman colony. He renamed it Aelia Capitolina and dedicated it to the god Jupiter. During this time, Jews were prohibited from entering the city, and Jerusalem took on a typical Roman urban layout with a main street known as the Cardo Maximus.

The Byzantine Transformation

The city underwent a significant transformation in the fourth century when the Roman Empire embraced Christianity. Under Emperor Constantine the Great and his mother Helena, Jerusalem became a primary destination for pilgrims. The construction of the Church of the Holy Sepulchre marked the beginning of a golden age for the city as a Christian spiritual center.

During the Byzantine era, the ban on Jewish residents was partially maintained, though the city flourished with the construction of numerous churches and monasteries. Emperor Justinian I further enhanced the city by expanding the Cardo and building the massive Nea Church, making Jerusalem one of the most prominent cities in the Byzantine East.

End of Byzantine Control

Byzantine rule was briefly interrupted by a Persian invasion in the early seventh century, which caused significant damage to many religious sites. Although Emperor Heraclius managed to recover the city shortly after, the Byzantine administration did not last long. In six hundred thirty eight AD, the city was surrendered to the Arab forces under Caliph Umar, ending the long period of Greco Roman governance.
