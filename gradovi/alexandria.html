The City of Alexandria

Alexandria Under Roman and Byzantine Rule

The Roman Period and Imperial Status

After the death of Cleopatra in thirty BC, Alexandria became part of the Roman Empire as the capital of the province of Egypt. It was governed directly by the emperor through a prefect, reflecting its immense importance as the primary granary of Rome. The city remained the second most important metropolis in the ancient world, surpassed only by Rome itself in population and grandeur.

During the Roman principate, Alexandria was home to a diverse population of Greeks, Egyptians, and Jews. It continued to be a world class center for learning and science, centered around the Great Library and the Museum. Emperors like Hadrian visited the city and commissioned many public works, although the city also experienced periods of unrest and significant damage during various revolts and imperial campaigns.

A Major Center of Early Christianity

Alexandria played a pivotal role in the development of Christian theology and administration. It was the seat of the Patriarchate of Alexandria, one of the most powerful positions in the early church. The city was the site of intense intellectual debates and was home to famous theologians such as Origen and Athanasius. This spiritual authority persisted well into the Byzantine era, making the city a pillar of the Eastern Roman Empire.

The Byzantine Era and Religious Life

As the Roman Empire transitioned into the Byzantine period, Alexandria remained a vital economic and cultural hub. The city was famous for its majestic churches and its role in the grain trade that sustained Constantinople. Despite facing challenges like the devastating tsunami in three hundred sixty five AD and frequent religious conflicts, it maintained its status as a premier Mediterranean port.

Under the rule of Justinian I, the city continued to flourish as a center of Byzantine administration. The Great Library might have faded, but the intellectual life of the city continued through private scholars and religious institutions. The iconic Lighthouse of Alexandria, the Pharos, still stood during this time as a symbol of the enduring legacy of the city.

The Persian Invasion and Arab Conquest

The later years of Byzantine rule were marked by external threats. In six hundred nineteen AD, the Sassanid Persians captured Alexandria and held it for a decade before Emperor Heraclius managed to reclaim it. However, Byzantine control was soon challenged again by the rising power of the Arab Caliphate. After a long siege, the city fell to Arab forces in six hundred forty one AD, which brought an end to over nine centuries of Greco Roman and Byzantine governance.
