Glycerius

Glycerius: The Commander Who Became a Bishop

Glycerius was one of the last men to hold the title of Western Roman Emperor, ruling from 473 to 474. Before he was elevated to the throne, he served as the commander of the imperial guard, a position that brought him close to the center of power during a very unstable era. His rise to the purple was orchestrated by Gundobad, a powerful Burgundian general who needed a stable figurehead to lead the remains of the empire after the death of the previous emperor, Olybrius.

Diplomacy as a Shield

Although his reign was short, Glycerius proved to be a surprisingly capable leader when it came to protecting Italy. He faced immediate threats from barbarian groups like the Visigoths and the Ostrogoths who were looking to expand their territories. Instead of engaging in costly and risky battles, Glycerius used clever diplomacy and strategic payments. He managed to divert a Visigothic invasion toward Gaul and famously paid the Ostrogothic king two thousand gold coins to lead his people away from the Italian heartland.

Despite these successes in keeping Italy safe, Glycerius never gained the support of the Eastern Roman Empire. The court in Constantinople viewed him as an illegitimate puppet of a barbarian general. Consequently, Emperor Leo I refused to recognize him and instead sent his own candidate, Julius Nepos, with an army to claim the Western throne. In a turning point for Glycerius, his main protector Gundobad left Italy to claim a throne of his own in Burgundy, leaving the emperor completely isolated and without military support.

From Imperial Palace to the Church of Salona

When Julius Nepos arrived near Rome in the summer of 474, Glycerius realized that his time as emperor was over. Rather than causing a bloody civil war, he chose to abdicate peacefully. In an unusual turn of events for a deposed ruler of that time, he was not executed. Instead, he was ordained as a priest and sent to the province of Dalmatia to serve as the Bishop of Salona, located near the modern city of Split.

Glycerius lived out the rest of his life in Dalmatia, where he transitioned from a political figure to a religious one. Some historical accounts even hint that he may have played a role in the eventual assassination of Julius Nepos, the man who took his throne, though these stories remain a mystery of the past. His journey from the highest office in the Roman world to a bishop's seat in Salona perfectly captures the fading power of the Western emperors and the rising influence of the Church.
