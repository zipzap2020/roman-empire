Geta

Geta: The Erased Emperor

Publius Septimius Geta was the younger son of Septimius Severus and the tragic co-ruler of the Roman Empire alongside his brother Caracalla. While history often remembers them as a pair, they were bitter rivals from childhood. Geta’s short reign in 211 AD was less a period of governance and more a desperate struggle for survival within the walls of the imperial palace. His life ended in one of the most famous acts of fratricide in antiquity, followed by a systematic attempt to scrub his very existence from the record of human history.

A House Divided

Even before their father died, the tension between the two brothers was so great that Severus reportedly took them both to Britain to keep them busy with military campaigns. When Severus died in 211 AD, he left the empire to both sons equally. They returned to Rome, but the city was effectively split in two.

They divided the sprawling imperial palace on the Palatine Hill, blocking off corridors and reinforcing doors to prevent the other from staging an assassination. They never ate together and surrounded themselves with separate bodies of guards. There was even a serious proposal to physically split the Roman Empire in two, with Geta ruling the East from Antioch or Alexandria and Caracalla ruling the West from Rome. This plan was only scrapped when their mother, Julia Domna, tearfully protested that she could not be divided between them.

Death in a Mother's Arms

The stalemate could not last. In late December 211 AD, Caracalla convinced their mother to invite both brothers to her private apartments for a secret reconciliation. Geta, trusting in the presence of his mother, came unarmed and without his guards.

As soon as he entered the room, centurions loyal to Caracalla burst from their hiding places. Geta realized the trap and threw himself into his mother’s arms, begging for protection. The soldiers struck him down anyway, and he died soaked in her blood. Caracalla reportedly rushed from the room to the Praetorian camp, claiming he had narrowly escaped an assassination attempt by his brother.

Damnatio Memoriae

What followed Geta’s death was the most thorough "Damnatio Memoriae" (condemnation of memory) ever executed by a Roman ruler. Caracalla made it a capital offense to even speak Geta's name. Thousands of his friends, advisors, and even the poets who had written about him were executed in a massive purge.

Across the entire Roman world, stone masons were sent to public monuments to chisel Geta’s name and image out of the stone. His face was scraped off painted portraits and his name was hammered out of bronze inscriptions. On the Arch of Septimius Severus in the Roman Forum, you can still see the blank spaces and uneven stone where Geta’s titles were once proudly displayed alongside his father’s and brother’s.
