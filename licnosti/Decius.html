Decius

Decius: The Restorer of Tradition

Gaius Messius Quintus Traianus Decius was a man of the old guard. Born in Pannonia, he was a distinguished senator and general who viewed the chaos of the 3rd century as a direct result of Rome’s abandonment of its ancient virtues and gods. When he seized power from Philip the Arab in 249 AD, he did so with a mission to return the empire to its glorious roots. However, his short reign from 249 to 251 AD is remembered less for its restoration and more for two grim milestones: the first empire wide persecution of Christians and the first time a Roman emperor was killed in battle by a foreign enemy.

The Edict of Decius

Decius believed that the "Pax Deorum" (the peace of the gods) had been broken. To fix it, he issued an edict in 250 AD requiring every citizen in the empire to perform a sacrifice to the state gods and the well being of the emperor. This was not a direct attack on Christianity specifically, but it created a massive crisis for the growing church.

Those who performed the sacrifice received a "libellus," a certificate proving their loyalty. Christians who refused faced imprisonment, torture, or execution. This period produced many famous martyrs, including Pope Fabian, but it also created deep divisions within the Christian community between those who stood firm and those who gave in to save their lives.

The Gothic Threat

While Decius was busy trying to fix the spiritual heart of Rome, the northern borders were collapsing. A massive confederation of Goths, led by their king Cniva, crossed the Danube and began ravaging the Balkan provinces. Decius, true to his image as a soldier emperor, took his son Herennius Etruscus and led the legions north to meet the threat personally.

The campaign was grueling. Decius suffered a major setback at the Battle of Beroe, marking the first time a Roman emperor had been forced to flee from barbarians. He regrouped and managed to trap the Goths as they tried to retreat with their loot, leading to a final, desperate confrontation in the swamps of Moesia.

The Disaster at Abritus

In June 251 AD, the two armies met at the Battle of Abritus. Early in the fighting, Decius's son was killed by an arrow. When the soldiers tried to console the Emperor, he reportedly replied that "the loss of one soldier is of little importance to the republic."

However, the battle turned into a catastrophe. The Roman legions became bogged down in the marshy terrain, and the Goths slaughtered them. Both Decius and his son perished in the swamp. Their bodies were never recovered. It was a shocking blow to the Roman psyche; the man who had set out to restore Rome's ancient majesty had instead led its army to a humiliating and unprecedented defeat.
