Macrinus

Macrinus: The Equestrian Outsider

Marcus Opellius Macrinus was a trailblazer in Roman history, though perhaps not by choice. His reign from 217 to 218 AD was the first time a man became emperor without first having attained the rank of Senator. As an Equestrian and the Praetorian Prefect under Caracalla, Macrinus was a legal expert and a bureaucrat rather than a blue blooded aristocrat. His rise to power proved that the old social hierarchies of Rome were dissolving, replaced by a system where proximity to the emperor and control of the guards was the only currency that mattered.

The Reluctant Assassin

Macrinus’s path to the throne was born out of pure survival. While serving Caracalla during the Parthian campaign, he reportedly intercepted a prophecy or a letter suggesting the Emperor intended to execute him. Knowing Caracalla’s volatile nature, Macrinus organized a small conspiracy among the soldiers. After the Emperor was stabbed to death during a roadside stop, Macrinus spent three days feigning grief before "reluctantly" accepting the purple from the leaderless army.

Because he remained in the East to conclude the war with Parthia, Macrinus never actually set foot in Rome as Emperor. This distance made it difficult for him to win over the Senate, who viewed him as a "low born" interloper, or the Roman people, who had no personal connection to him.

The Humiliation at Nisibis

The defining failure of Macrinus's reign was his handling of the Parthian Empire. The Parthians, seeking revenge for Caracalla’s unprovoked invasions, met the Roman army at the Battle of Nisibis. The fighting was long and bloody, but neither side achieved a decisive victory.

Fearing a prolonged war and his own soldiers' wavering loyalty, Macrinus opted for diplomacy. He paid a massive indemnity of 200 million sesterces to the Parthian King Artabanus IV to secure peace. To the Roman legions, who were used to the aggressive conquests of Severus and Caracalla, this looked like a shameful act of cowardice and a waste of the empire's wealth.

The Rise of the Boy King

The Severan women, led by the formidable Julia Maesa (the sister of Julia Domna), saw Macrinus's weakness as an opportunity to restore their family to power. From their base in Emesa, Syria, they spread a rumor that Maesa's fourteen year old grandson, Elagabalus, was actually the secret illegitimate son of the beloved Caracalla.

The Legio III Gallica, stationed nearby, was easily bribed with the Severan family's vast wealth and declared for the boy. Macrinus attempted to fight back, but his troops deserted him at the Battle of Antioch. He fled toward Rome disguised as a courier, but he was recognized, captured at Chalcedon, and executed. His fourteen month reign ended as it began: in a flurry of military desertion and political desperation.
