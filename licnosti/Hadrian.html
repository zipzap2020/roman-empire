Hadrian

Hadrian: The Restless Traveler

Publius Aelius Hadrianus, the third of the "Five Good Emperors," was a man of complex contradictions. While his predecessor Trajan was a conqueror who expanded Rome to its breaking point, Hadrian was a consolidator who spent more than half of his twenty one year reign (117 to 138 AD) traveling the provinces. He was a brilliant architect, a poet, and a scholar of Greek culture, but he was also a stern disciplinarian who could be ruthlessly efficient when he felt the security of the empire was at stake.

Drawing the Line: Hadrian’s Wall

One of Hadrian’s first acts was to abandon Trajan’s recent conquests in Mesopotamia, which he deemed impossible to defend. He shifted Roman policy from expansion to defense. Nowhere is this more visible than in Northern Britain, where he ordered the construction of a seventy three mile long stone wall.

Hadrian’s Wall was not just a fence; it was a sophisticated military zone with forts every few miles. It served as a physical manifestation of the Empire’s limits and a way to control trade and migration. To ensure the legions remained sharp during this era of relative peace, Hadrian visited the camps personally, eating the same basic rations as the common soldiers and marching twenty miles a day in full armor.

The Architect of the Pantheon

Hadrian was a visionary builder who deeply influenced the skyline of Rome. His most famous project was the reconstruction of the Pantheon. He designed it with a massive concrete dome—the largest in the world for over a millennium—with a circular opening at the top known as the "oculus."

In Tibur (modern Tivoli), he built a sprawling villa that acted as a laboratory for his architectural experiments. It was a collection of buildings inspired by the famous sites he had seen during his travels in Greece and Egypt. This retreat allowed him to escape the stifling politics of the capital, which he found increasingly tiresome.

The Bar Kokhba Revolt and Personal Loss

Hadrian’s love for Greek culture often blinded him to the sensibilities of other peoples. His decision to build a temple to Jupiter on the site of the Jewish Temple in Jerusalem sparked the Bar Kokhba Revolt in 132 AD. The resulting three year war was one of the most brutal in Roman history, leading to the near total destruction of Judea and the displacement of the Jewish people.

His personal life was marked by the tragic death of Antinous, a beautiful youth he loved deeply. When Antinous drowned in the Nile, Hadrian was inconsolable; he founded the city of Antinoöpolis in his honor and had him deified, filling the empire with statues of the young man.

Hadrian died in 138 AD after a long and painful illness. He chose Antoninus Pius as his successor, on the condition that Antoninus in turn adopt the young Marcus Aurelius. This careful planning ensured that his policy of stable, defensive government would continue for another generation.
