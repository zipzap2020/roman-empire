Tacitus: The Last Choice of the Senate

Marcus Claudius Tacitus was an elderly senator who found himself at the helm of the Roman Empire in late 275 AD. Following the shocking assassination of Aurelian, the Roman army—filled with remorse for killing their greatest leader—did something unprecedented: they asked the Senate to choose the next emperor. After a period of hesitation that lasted several months, the seventy five year old Tacitus was elected. His brief reign represented a final, fleeting hope that the old traditions of senatorial rule could coexist with the new reality of a military dominated world.

The Reluctant Aristocrat

Tacitus was a man of immense wealth and prestige. He claimed, likely falsely, to be a descendant of the famous 1st century historian Tacitus. When the Senate approached him, he initially tried to decline, citing his advanced age and failing health. However, the political vacuum was too dangerous to ignore.

Once he accepted the purple, he attempted to restore the Senate's lost influence. He gave the body the power to appoint governors and hear appeals, moving away from the autocratic style of the soldier emperors. To the traditionalists in Rome, it felt as though the Republic might finally be breathing again.

War in the East

Despite his age, Tacitus could not stay in the comfort of Rome. A group of Gothic mercenaries, originally hired by Aurelian for the Persian campaign, had found themselves without a leader or a mission. They began pillaging the provinces of Asia Minor, reaching as far as Cilicia.

Tacitus, showing surprising vigor, traveled to the East with his brother Florianus. He managed to win a significant victory over the Goths, for which he was granted the title *Gothicus Maximus*. It seemed as though the "scholar emperor" might actually be able to handle the military demands of the era.

A Sudden Collapse

The success was short lived. In mid 276 AD, only six months into his reign, Tacitus died in the city of Tyana. The cause of his death is a subject of debate. Some sources claim he was assassinated by the same disgruntled soldiers who had murdered the governor of Syria, while others suggest he simply succumbed to a fever brought on by the exhaustion of the campaign and the harsh climate.

With his death, the Senate’s brief experiment in choosing the emperor came to a permanent end. His brother Florianus immediately seized power, but he was soon challenged by a far more formidable soldier named Probus. The age of the civilian emperor was dead for good, and the Illyrian generals were ready to take the reins once more.
