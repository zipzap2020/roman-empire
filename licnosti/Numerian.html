Numerian: The Tragic Scholar

Marcus Aurelius Numerianus, known as Numerian, was the younger son of Carus and perhaps the most gentle soul to wear the imperial purple during the violent 3rd century. Unlike the battle hardened generals who typically seized power, Numerian was a celebrated poet and orator, once winning a prestigious prize for his literary skills. When his father died during the Persian campaign in 283 AD, the young man was left in command of a retreating army, a task for which he was temperamentally and physically unprepared. His short reign ended in one of the most bizarre and macabre mysteries in Roman history.

A Nightmare Retreat

After the sudden death of Carus—allegedly by a bolt of lightning—Numerian was proclaimed co emperor alongside his brother Carinus, who remained in the West. The army, terrified by the "divine omen" of the lightning strike, demanded an immediate retreat from Persian territory.

The withdrawal was a disaster. The soldiers were exhausted, and Numerian began to suffer from a severe eye infection, likely brought on by the dust and heat of the desert. He was forced to travel in a closed litter, shielded from the sun and the eyes of his men. The true power during this journey fell into the hands of his father in law, the Praetorian Prefect Aper.

The Emperor in the Litter

For weeks as the army marched toward the Bosporus, the soldiers were told that Numerian was alive but too ill to be seen. Aper issued orders in the Emperor's name and assured the troops that the young ruler would address them once they reached the safety of Roman territory.

However, as the army reached the city of Nicomedia, the smell of decay coming from the imperial litter became impossible to ignore. When the soldiers finally forced the doors open, they found not a sick emperor, but a rotting corpse. Numerian had been dead for days, perhaps weeks, while Aper continued to rule from behind the curtains of the litter.

The Rise of Diocletian

The discovery of the body sparked an immediate outcry. A council of generals and tribunes was formed to investigate the death and choose a successor. They arrested Aper and brought him before the assembly.

The commander of the imperial bodyguards, a man named Diocles, stood before the troops. In a dramatic show of justice, he accused Aper of murdering Numerian to seize the throne. Without waiting for a trial, Diocles drew his sword and killed Aper on the spot. Whether Diocles was truly avenging a murder or simply eliminating the only man who knew the truth about Numerian's death is still debated by historians. Regardless, the soldiers hailed Diocles as their new emperor, and he took the name **Diocletian**.
