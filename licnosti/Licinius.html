Licinius

Licinius: The Last Rival of Constantine

Valerius Licinianus Licinius was a battle hardened soldier from Dacia who rose to the highest levels of power through his friendship with the Emperor Galerius. Unlike the sons of emperors who claimed power through blood, Licinius was a "professional" pick, officially appointed as Augustus in 308 AD to bring order to the crumbling Tetrarchy. For over a decade, he ruled the Eastern half of the empire, at times acting as a close ally to Constantine the Great. However, their relationship was a ticking time bomb of ambition. Their rivalry eventually turned into a struggle for the soul of the empire, pitting the old Roman traditions against the rising tide of Christianity.

The Marriage and the Edict

In 313 AD, Licinius and Constantine met in Milan to carve up the Roman world. To cement their alliance, Licinius married Constantine’s half sister, Constantia. Together, they issued the famous **Edict of Milan**, which granted full legal recognition to Christianity and returned confiscated property to the Church.

While Constantine is often given all the credit, Licinius was an equal partner in this religious revolution. Shortly after the meeting, Licinius rushed East to defeat his rival Maximinus Daza, becoming the undisputed master of the Balkan and Eastern provinces. For a few years, it seemed as though the empire might finally find peace under two shared rulers.

The War of Two Brothers-in-Law

The peace was a facade. Constantine was a man who could not share power, and Licinius was a stubborn traditionalist who grew increasingly suspicious of Constantine’s growing influence. Between 314 and 324 AD, the two men engaged in a series of civil wars that stretched from the Balkans to the gates of Byzantium.

As the conflict intensified, Licinius began to backtrack on the religious freedom he had helped establish. He banned church synods and purged Christians from his administration, fearing they were a "fifth column" loyal to Constantine. This allowed Constantine to frame the war not just as a political struggle, but as a holy crusade to liberate the East from a pagan tyrant.

The Siege of Byzantium and the End

The end came at the **Battle of Chrysopolis** in 324 AD. Licinius’s veteran legions were smashed by Constantine’s forces. Licinius fled to Nicomedia, where his wife, Constantia, pleaded with her brother to spare her husband's life. Constantine agreed, stripping Licinius of his purple robes and sending him to live as a private citizen in Thessalonica.

The mercy did not last long. In 325 AD, claiming that Licinius was plotting with barbarian tribes to regain his throne, Constantine ordered him strangled. With the death of Licinius, the last vestiges of the Tetrarchy were swept away. For the first time in nearly forty years, the Roman Empire was united under a single ruler, and the path was cleared for the official Christianization of the Roman state.
