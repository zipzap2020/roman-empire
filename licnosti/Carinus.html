Carinus

Carinus: The Last of the Old Chaos

Marcus Aurelius Carinus was the elder son of Carus and perhaps one of the most maligned figures in Roman history. While his father and brother were away fighting the Persians in the East, Carinus was left in charge of the West. He was a capable general who successfully defended the Rhine and Danube, but his personal reputation was a disaster. Chroniclers portrayed him as a man of bottomless vice who turned the imperial palace into a den of excess. Whether he was truly a monster or simply the victim of the ultimate "winner's history" written by his successor, his death marked the final gasp of the chaotic 3rd century.

The Black Legend of the West

Historical accounts, which were often written by those loyal to the man who overthrew him, paint a grim picture of Carinus in Rome. He was accused of marrying and divorcing nine different women, executing innocent senators on a whim, and filling high government offices with his personal drinking buddies.

Despite these stories of decadence, Carinus was no coward. In 284 AD, he successfully defeated the usurper Julianus in northern Italy. He possessed the military loyalty and the resources to hold the West, but the mysterious death of his brother Numerian in the East brought a far more dangerous challenger to his doorstep: a commander named Diocles, better known as Diocletian.

The Battle of the Margus

In 285 AD, the two massive Roman armies—the Western legions of Carinus and the Eastern legions of Diocletian—met at the Margus River in modern day Serbia. It was one of the largest and most significant clashes of the era.

Tactically, Carinus was winning the battle. His Western troops were pushing back Diocletian’s lines, and it seemed as though the "vicious" emperor would secure his throne. However, at the moment of his greatest triumph, his own officers turned on him. According to the most famous version of the story, Carinus was murdered in the middle of the battle by a tribune whose wife he had seduced. With Carinus dead, his army immediately surrendered, and Diocletian was left as the undisputed master of the Roman world.

The End of an Era

The death of Carinus was the closing chapter of the Crisis of the Third Century. For fifty years, the empire had been a revolving door of soldier emperors and usurpers. By defeating the last of the house of Carus, Diocletian was able to sweep away the old, broken system.

Carinus was the last emperor to rule in the traditional "Barracks" style. His successor would go on to reinvent the government entirely, splitting the empire into the Tetrarchy and moving Rome into a new age of stability and rigid bureaucracy known as the Dominate.
