Valerian

Valerian: The Emperor in Chains

Publius Licinius Valerianus was a man of impeccable noble lineage who came to power in 253 AD during a period of total systemic collapse. Unlike the "barracks" generals who had preceded him, Valerian was a seasoned statesman and a favorite of the Senate. He recognized that the empire was too vast and the threats too numerous for one man to handle, so he took the radical step of appointing his son, Gallienus, as co emperor. Valerian headed east to face the Persians while Gallienus stayed west to hold the Rhine. It was a logical plan that ended in the single most humiliating moment in Roman history.

Persecution and Political Pressure

Valerian inherited an empire decimated by the Plague of Cyprian and constant border raids. Like Decius before him, he believed that the favor of the gods was the only way to save Rome. In 257 and 258 AD, he issued harsh edicts specifically targeting the Christian hierarchy.

Unlike previous persecutions, Valerian’s orders focused on the execution of bishops and priests and the confiscation of church property. It was during this time that famous figures like Saint Lawrence and Pope Sixtus II were martyred. Valerian was not necessarily a religious fanatic, but a desperate politician trying to find a scapegoat for a string of military and economic disasters.

The Disaster at Edessa

The Sassanid King Shapur I had been carving through the Roman East with terrifying efficiency. Valerian gathered a massive force of 70,000 men and marched to meet him. However, his army was soon crippled by the plague and surrounded by the superior Persian cavalry near the city of Edessa.

In 260 AD, for the first and only time in history, a Roman Emperor was taken prisoner by a foreign power. During a parley intended to negotiate peace, Valerian was seized by Shapur’s guards. The Roman army, leaderless and sick, surrendered in mass.

A Life in Captivity

The fate of Valerian after his capture became the stuff of legend and propaganda. Persian sources claim he was treated with respect, but Roman and later Christian writers told a much darker story. They claimed that Shapur used the aged emperor as a human mounting block to step onto his horse.

The most famous, and perhaps most gruesome, account states that after Valerian died in captivity, Shapur had him flayed. His skin was supposedly dyed red, stuffed with straw, and kept in a Persian temple as a permanent trophy of Roman humiliation. Whether this is true or a piece of Roman "horror" fiction, the political reality was clear: the Roman Emperor was a captive, and the empire was effectively headless.

The Empire Splinters

Valerian’s capture triggered the absolute low point of the 3rd century. With the senior emperor in chains and his son Gallienus struggling to keep control in the West, the empire began to break apart. Two breakaway states formed: the Gallic Empire in the west and the Palmyrene Empire in the east. Rome was no longer a single entity, but a collection of warring fragments.
