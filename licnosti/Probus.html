Probus

Probus: The Disciplined Builder

Marcus Aurelius Probus was the next in the line of great Illyrian soldier emperors who refused to let Rome die. Taking the throne in 276 AD after the brief and bloody transition from Tacitus, Probus spent his six year reign in a state of perpetual motion. He was a man of legendary discipline who believed that a soldier should never be idle. When he wasn't crushing barbarian invasions on the Rhine and Danube, he was putting his legions to work planting vineyards and digging canals. He successfully maintained the unity Aurelian had fought for, but his insistence on hard labor eventually pushed his exhausted troops past their breaking point.

The Scourge of the Germanic Tribes

The early years of Probus’s reign were defined by some of the most successful military operations of the century. Gaul had been devastated by the Franks and Alamanni, who had occupied dozens of Roman cities. Probus led a brilliant campaign that not only drove the invaders back across the Rhine but also saw Roman troops crossing the river to take the fight into German territory.

He reportedly killed 400,000 invaders during these campaigns and forced the surviving tribes to return their loot and provide recruits for the Roman army. To secure the border, he rebuilt the fortifications along the Rhine, creating a temporary era of peace for the western provinces that had suffered so much during the breakaway Gallic Empire.

Guns and Grapes

Probus is perhaps best remembered for his unusual domestic policy. He hated seeing his soldiers sit around in winter quarters, fearing that boredom led to mutiny. He famously declared that the day would come when the empire no longer needed a standing army.

In the meantime, he utilized his men as a massive public works department. He used the legions to plant vast vineyards in Gaul, Pannonia, and Moesia, effectively lifting the imperial ban on wine production in those regions. Many of the famous wine regions in modern day France and Germany can trace their viticultural roots back to the labor of Probus’s soldiers.

The Mutiny at Sirmium

In 282 AD, while Probus was at his birthplace of Sirmium preparing for a campaign against the Persians, his luck ran out. The troops, already tired of agricultural labor and heavy construction, heard that the Praetorian Prefect, Carus, had been proclaimed emperor by the legions in Raetia.

Probus ordered his men to march against the usurper, but they had finally had enough. Instead of fighting Carus, they turned on Probus. He sought refuge in a high iron tower he had built for observation, but the soldiers stormed it and murdered him. Even though they killed him, the Romans later realized his worth, and he was deified by the Senate. He had been a stern and demanding master, but he left the empire significantly stronger than he found it.
