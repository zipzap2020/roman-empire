Carus: The Lightning Strike from the West

Marcus Aurelius Carus was a man in a hurry. Rising to power in 282 AD after the murder of Probus, he was already sixty years old, a grandfather, and a seasoned veteran of the imperial administration. Unlike his predecessors, he didn't wait for the Senate’s approval. He immediately appointed his two sons, Carinus and Numerian, as his heirs to ensure a stable dynasty. Carus was determined to finish what Aurelian and Probus had started: the total humiliation of the Sassanid Persian Empire.

The Drive to the East

Carus left his older son Carinus to manage the western provinces and took his younger, more literary son Numerian with him to the East. The timing was perfect. The Sassanid Persians were distracted by internal civil wars, leaving their western flank vulnerable.

Carus marched through Mesopotamia with a speed that shocked the world. He defeated the Persians in a pitched battle and achieved what many emperors had failed to do: he captured the Persian capital of Ctesiphon. For the first time in decades, a Roman Emperor was standing in the heart of the enemy's empire, seemingly on the verge of total victory.

The Bolt from the Blue

In July 283 AD, at the very height of his success, Carus’s life came to a bizarre and sudden end. While his army was camped near the Tigris River, a violent thunderstorm rolled across the plains. According to the official reports, the Emperor’s tent was struck by lightning, and he was found dead amidst the smoke and flames.

Many modern historians are skeptical of the lightning story, suggesting it may have been a cover for a more traditional assassination led by his ambitious Praetorian Prefect, Aper. However, to the superstitious Roman soldiers, the lightning was a clear sign that the gods did not want the army to march any further into Persia. The campaign was immediately halted.

A Dynasty in Danger

The death of Carus left the empire in the hands of his two sons. Numerian, who was with the army in the East, was forced to lead a long and difficult retreat back toward Roman territory. Meanwhile, Carinus was in Rome, quickly earning a reputation as a cruel and self-indulgent ruler. The unity that Carus had worked so hard to establish began to dissolve before his body was even cold, setting the stage for the final act of the Crisis of the Third Century.
