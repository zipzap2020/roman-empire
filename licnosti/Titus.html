Titus

Titus: The Delight of Mankind

Titus Flavius Vespasianus was the second ruler of the Flavian dynasty and the first Roman Emperor to succeed his own biological father. Though his reign lasted only two short years from 79 to 81 AD, he earned a reputation as one of the most benevolent and beloved leaders in the history of the Empire. This was a dramatic transformation for a man who, as a young general, had been feared for his ruthlessness during the Jewish War and his perceived potential to become another Nero.

The Conqueror of Jerusalem

Titus rose to prominence under his father’s command, eventually taking full control of the Roman forces in Judaea. In 70 AD, he successfully concluded the Siege of Jerusalem, a brutal conflict that resulted in the destruction of the Second Temple. The spoils from this campaign, including the legendary Menorah, were paraded through Rome in a massive triumph and used to fund the construction of the Colosseum.

To commemorate this victory, the Arch of Titus was later erected in the Roman Forum. Despite the violence of the war, as Emperor, Titus proved to be surprisingly merciful, ending treason trials and refusing to punish those who spoke out against him.

The Year of Disasters

Titus’s brief reign was tested by a series of catastrophic events that would have broken a lesser leader. Only two months after he took office, Mount Vesuvius erupted, burying the cities of Pompeii and Herculaneum. Titus personally coordinated the relief efforts, donating large sums from the imperial treasury to help survivors and visiting the devastated region twice.

Hardly had the dust settled from the volcano when a massive fire broke out in Rome, burning for three days and nights, followed immediately by one of the worst plagues in the city's history. Throughout these crises, Titus remained highly visible and generous, earning the nickname "Amor ac deliciae generis humani"—the darling and delight of mankind.

Completion of the Colosseum and Early Death

In 80 AD, Titus officially inaugurated the Flavian Amphitheatre, completing the project his father had started. He hosted one hundred days of lavish games, which included naval battles in the flooded arena and the appearance of thousands of wild animals.

His reign came to a sudden and tragic end in 81 AD when he fell ill with a fever, possibly at the same villa where his father had died. His final words were reportedly a cryptic lament: "I have made but one mistake." While historians still debate what he meant, many believe he was referring to his failure to prevent his younger brother, Domitian, from succeeding him. Titus died at the age of 41, leaving a legacy of compassion that stood in stark contrast to the dark years of his brother's reign that followed.
