Vitellius

Vitellius: The Gluttonous Emperor

Aulus Vitellius was the third man to claim the throne during the chaotic "Year of the Four Emperors." While his predecessors were defined by their sternness or their noble ends, Vitellius is remembered by history primarily for his vices. He was an easygoing but weak willed man whose primary interests were the kitchen and the tavern rather than the halls of power. His eight month reign in 69 AD was a period of administrative paralysis and military decadence that ended in one of the most brutal spectacles of violence the city of Rome had ever seen.

The Rhine Revolt

Vitellius owed his rise not to his own ambition, but to the frustrations of the Rhine legions. These battle hardened soldiers felt neglected by Galba and were eager for the rewards that came with backing a successful emperor. Vitellius was their commander in Germania Inferior, and his indulgent nature made him popular with the troops. In January 69, they saluted him as emperor.

Vitellius himself did not lead the march into Italy; he followed behind his generals, Valens and Caecina. By the time he arrived in Rome, Otho was dead and the city was occupied by his victorious, rowdy soldiers. Vitellius entered the city not as a restorer of order, but as a conqueror, allowing his troops to roam the streets and drain the resources of the capital.

A Reign of Excess

Once settled in the imperial palace, Vitellius famously neglected the duties of the state. Suetonius and other historians claim he held at least three massive banquets every day, costing the empire millions of sesterces. He was known for his "Shield of Minerva" dish, a massive platter containing pike livers, pheasant brains, and peacock tongues gathered from across the known world.

While the Emperor feasted, the foundations of his power were crumbling. In the East, the legions engaged in the Jewish War had proclaimed their own general, Vespasian, as emperor. The Danubian legions quickly followed suit. Vitellius, paralyzed by indecision and distracted by his luxuries, failed to prepare for the inevitable counterattack.

The Gemonian Stairs

The end came in December 69 AD when Vespasian’s forces, led by Antonius Primus, broke into Rome. A desperate and bloody battle was fought in the streets and even in the Temple of Jupiter on the Capitoline Hill, which was burned to the ground. Vitellius attempted to flee in disguise, hiding in a doorkeeper's lodge and barricading the door with a bed and a mattress.

He was eventually dragged out by Flavian soldiers. With his hands tied behind his back and a noose around his neck, he was led through the Forum while the mob pelted him with filth and insults. He was tortured and finally killed on the Gemonian Stairs, the place where the bodies of criminals were traditionally thrown. His last words were a final spark of defiance: "Yet I was once your Emperor." His body was dragged with a hook into the Tiber, clearing the way for Vespasian and the start of the Flavian dynasty.
