Tullus Hostilius

Tullus Hostilius: The Warlike King

Tullus Hostilius was the third King of Rome and the polar opposite of his predecessor, the peaceful Numa. If Numa sought to civilize Rome through religion, Tullus sought to expand it through fire and iron. His reign was defined by his obsession with military glory and his disdain for religious ceremonies, a trait that the Romans believed eventually led to his spectacular and divine destruction.

The Destruction of Alba Longa

The most famous event of Tullus’s reign was the war with Alba Longa, Rome’s mother city. Rather than a full scale battle between two massive armies, the conflict was settled by a legendary duel between two sets of triplets: the Roman Horatii and the Alban Curiatii.

After a brutal fight that left only one Roman brother standing, Rome claimed victory. However, when the Alban leader Mettius Fufetius later betrayed Rome during a battle against the Etruscans, Tullus showed his legendary ruthlessness. He had Mettius torn apart by chariots and ordered the total destruction of Alba Longa. He forced the entire population to move to Rome, doubling the city's size and settling the newcomers on the Caelian Hill.

The Curia Hostilia

Despite his violent nature, Tullus contributed to Rome’s political infrastructure. To accommodate the influx of new noble families from the conquered Alba Longa, he built a new, permanent meeting place for the Senate. This building, known as the Curia Hostilia, remained the heart of Roman political life for centuries. It served as a physical reminder of how conquest fueled the growth and evolution of the Roman government.

Divine Wrath and a Fiery End

Tullus Hostilius’s luck eventually ran out. According to Roman tradition, a terrible plague struck the city, and the King himself fell ill. Believing he had angered the gods by neglecting the religious rituals established by Numa, Tullus attempted to perform a secret sacrifice to Jupiter Elicius.

However, because he performed the rite incorrectly or without a pure heart, the god was not appeased. A bolt of lightning struck the King's palace, reducing it to ashes and killing Tullus instantly. His death was seen as a divine warning that while military strength was necessary, it had to be balanced with "pietas," or duty to the gods. He was succeeded by Ancus Marcius, who attempted to restore the balance between the war like ways of Romulus and the peaceful ways of Numa.
