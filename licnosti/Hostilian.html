Hostilian: The Emperor of the Plague

Hostilian was the younger son of Decius and a tragic footnote in the bloody narrative of the 3rd century. When his father and older brother perished in the marshes of Abritus in 251 AD, Hostilian was left behind in Rome. His brief stint as emperor was not defined by the sword or political intrigue, but by a microscopic killer that proved more effective than any Gothic army. He was the only emperor in a forty year span to die of natural causes, though "natural" in this case meant a horrific and relentless pestilence.

A Divided Succession

After the disaster at Abritus, the legions on the Danube proclaimed their general, Trebonianus Gallus, as emperor. However, Rome was not ready to abandon the bloodline of Decius. To avoid a new civil war while the Goths were still ravaging the provinces, Gallus made a shrewd political move. He accepted Hostilian as his co-emperor, treating the boy as his own son to maintain a sense of continuity and legitimacy.

While Gallus dealt with the fallout of the Gothic treaty, Hostilian remained the face of the imperial family in the capital. He was elevated to the rank of Augustus, but he was little more than a symbol of a shattered era, a child emperor in a city gripped by fear.

The Plague of Cyprian

During Hostilian's short reign, a devastating pandemic known as the Plague of Cyprian swept through the empire. Named after the Bishop of Carthage who documented its horrors, the disease was likely a form of viral hemorrhagic fever or smallpox. At its peak, it was reported that 5,000 people were dying every day in Rome alone.

The plague decimated the Roman army, emptied cities, and crippled the economy. It also claimed the life of the young emperor. In late 251 AD, Hostilian succumbed to the disease. His death left Trebonianus Gallus as the sole ruler, but it also robbed the Roman people of the last direct link to the man who had promised to restore their ancient glory.

The Ghost of a Dynasty

Because he died of illness rather than at the hands of an assassin, Hostilian’s reputation remained untarnished. He was deified by the Senate, a rare honor in an age of usurpers. His death marked the final end of the Decian influence and left the empire entirely in the hands of the "Barracks" generals, who would continue to fight over the scraps of a plague stricken and war torn state.
