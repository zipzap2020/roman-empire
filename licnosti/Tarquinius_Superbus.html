Tarquinius Superbus

Tarquin the Proud: The Tyrant Who Ended a Kingdom

Lucius Tarquinius Superbus, known as Tarquin the Proud, was the seventh and final King of Rome. Unlike the kings before him who were elected or confirmed by the Senate, Tarquin seized the throne through the bloody assassination of his father in law, Servius Tullius. His reign was characterized by fear, heavy labor, and a total disregard for the traditions of the Roman people. He was the ultimate villain in the Roman national story, and his actions eventually pushed the population to a breaking point that changed the world forever.

Rule by Fear and Grandeur

Tarquin was a textbook autocrat. He diminished the power of the Senate by refusing to consult them and executed leading senators to seize their wealth. To keep the Roman mob occupied and to glorify his reign, he forced the plebeians into grueling manual labor. Under his watch, the massive Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus on the Capitoline Hill was finally completed, and the Cloaca Maxima was expanded into the sophisticated sewer system that would serve Rome for centuries.

While he was a tyrant at home, he was an effective military leader. He secured Rome's leadership over the Latin League and waged successful wars against the Volscians and the city of Gabii. However, his military success could not mask the growing resentment in Rome, as the citizens felt they were being treated more like slaves than Romans.

The Crime of Sextus Tarquinius

The spark that ignited the revolution did not come from the King himself, but from his son, Sextus Tarquinius. According to the legend, while the army was laying siege to the city of Ardea, the young nobles began to debate the virtues of their wives. They rode back to Rome to spy on them, finding only Lucretia, the wife of Lucius Tarquinius Collatinus, behaving with true Roman dignity and virtue.

Overcome by lust and jealousy, Sextus returned later and raped Lucretia at knifepoint. After he left, she summoned her father and husband, told them what had happened, and then took her own life to preserve her honor. This tragedy became the rallying cry for the Roman aristocracy.

The Birth of the Republic

Lucius Junius Brutus, a relative of the King who had been faking a simple mind to survive Tarquin’s purges, stepped forward and drew the bloody dagger from Lucretia’s body. He swore an oath to never again allow a king to rule in Rome. The citizens, moved by the sight of Lucretia's body and weary of Tarquin's oppression, voted to exile the royal family.

Tarquin attempted to regain his throne several times, even enlisting the help of the Etruscan King Lars Porsena, but the Romans held firm. In 509 BC, the monarchy was officially abolished and replaced by the Roman Republic. The power once held by a single king was divided between two annually elected consuls, ensuring that no one man could ever hold absolute power again. Tarquin died in exile, the last man to ever officially hold the title of King in Rome.
