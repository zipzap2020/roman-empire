Procopius

Procopius was a Roman usurper who made a desperate bid for the throne in the mid fourth century. A relative of Emperor Julian the Apostate on his mother's side, he was the last man to claim a blood connection to the prestigious dynasty of Constantine the Great. His rebellion against the brothers Valens and Valentinian I was a significant civil conflict that briefly threatened to split the empire once again.

From Fugitive to Emperor

Before his death in 363, Emperor Julian had reportedly intended for Procopius to be his successor, even entrusting him with a major command during the ill fated Persian campaign. However, when Julian died in battle, the army chose Jovian instead. Procopius, fearing for his life under the new administration, went into hiding. He lived as a fugitive for several years, moving between the fringes of the empire and the Black Sea region.

In 365, taking advantage of the unpopularity of Emperor Valens and the Emperor's absence from Constantinople, Procopius emerged from the shadows. He managed to bribe two legions passing through the city and used his family connections to win over the people. He was proclaimed emperor in the capital, presenting himself as the rightful heir to the Constantinian line, often appearing in public holding the young daughter of Constantius II to solidify his legitimacy.

Betrayal and Execution

The rebellion initially gained traction in Thrace and parts of Asia Minor, but it ultimately lacked the support of the senior military establishment. Valens, recovered from the initial shock, marched against him. The decisive moment came at the Battle of Thyatira in 366. Procopius was not defeated by military genius, but by the desertion of his own generals and troops, who were lured back to the side of Valens by promises of amnesty.

Abandoned by his followers, Procopius fled into the wilds of Phrygia but was captured and brought before Valens. The Emperor showed no mercy. Procopius was executed in a particularly brutal fashion; he was reportedly tied to two trees that had been bent toward each other and then released, tearing him apart. His death marked the final end of the Constantinian influence on the Roman throne and secured the rule of the Valentinianic dynasty.
