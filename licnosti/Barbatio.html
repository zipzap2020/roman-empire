Barbatio

Barbatio: The Opportunist General

Barbatio was a high ranking Roman officer who served during the reign of Emperor Constantius II in the mid fourth century. His career is often viewed through the lens of political intrigue rather than pure military merit. He rose to prominence not just through his leadership on the battlefield, but through his involvement in the dangerous internal politics of the imperial court, eventually reaching the rank of Magister Peditum, or Master of Foot.

Rise through Betrayal

Barbatio first made his mark as a commander of the household troops under Gallus Caesar, the Emperor’s cousin. When Constantius II became suspicious of Gallus and decided to remove him from power, Barbatio played a key role in the process. He was the one who arrested Gallus and stripped him of his imperial robes before his execution. For this act of loyalty to the Emperor, Barbatio was rewarded with a significant promotion, taking over the command previously held by the general Silvanus.

However, his military record was far from perfect. During a campaign against the Alamanni in 357, Barbatio was tasked with supporting the young Caesar Julian, who would later become Emperor. Instead of cooperating, Barbatio acted with a strange mix of incompetence and malice. He allowed a large group of barbarians to pass by his camp and even burned his own supply ships to prevent Julian from using them. This lack of coordination nearly sabotaged the Roman efforts, although Julian eventually managed to secure a victory at the Battle of Strasbourg despite Barbatio’s failures.

A Sudden Fall from Grace

In the paranoid atmosphere of Constantius II’s court, even the most loyal servants were never truly safe. Barbatio’s downfall came in 359 due to a private letter. His wife, Assyria, wrote to him suggesting that after the Emperor’s death, Barbatio might ascend to the throne. This letter was intercepted and brought to the attention of the Emperor.

In the eyes of Constantius, this was evidence of high treason. Barbatio and his wife were immediately arrested, tried, and beheaded. His story serves as a cautionary tale of the late Roman Empire, where the ability to navigate the treacherous waters of the imperial palace was just as important as the ability to lead an army, and where a single slip of the pen could lead to total ruin.
