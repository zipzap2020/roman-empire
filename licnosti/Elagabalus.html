Elagabalus

Elagabalus: The High Priest of Rome

Born Varius Avitus Bassianus, the teenager known as Elagabalus was perhaps the most eccentric and controversial figure to ever hold the title of Roman Emperor. His four year reign from 218 to 222 AD was not characterized by military campaigns or legal reforms, but by a radical attempt to replace the traditional Roman gods with a single Syrian sun deity, Elagabal. A fourteen year old boy thrust into power by his ambitious grandmother, Elagabalus shocked the Roman establishment with his gender bending behavior, his exotic religious rites, and his total disregard for the dignity of the imperial office.

The Sun King Comes to Rome

Elagabalus was the hereditary high priest of the sun god Elagabal in Emesa, Syria. When he arrived in Rome, he didn't leave his religion behind. He brought a massive conical black stone—the sacred baetyl of his god—and installed it in a magnificent new temple on the Palatine Hill called the Elagabalium.

He declared Elagabal to be the supreme deity of the Roman pantheon, even above Jupiter. He forced the leading senators to watch as he danced around the altar to the sound of Syrian drums and flutes. To create a divine marriage, he moved the sacred fire of Vesta to his new temple and took a Vestal Virgin as his wife, an act of sacrilege that horrified the Roman public.

Breaking Social Taboos

The young emperor's personal life was a constant source of scandal. Ancient historians, who were admittedly biased against him, claimed he preferred to be called a lady rather than a lord. He reportedly wore heavy makeup, silks, and jewelry, and allegedly offered massive rewards to any physician who could perform a primitive form of gender reassignment surgery.

He was also famous for his bizarre sense of humor and extravagant banquets. He supposedly smothered guests with thousands of rose petals dropped from the ceiling or served them "meals" made of glass and wood. While these stories may be exaggerated by his enemies, they reflect a ruler who was completely out of touch with the conservative, military-focused culture of the Roman elite.

The Grandmother's Betrayal

Julia Maesa, the grandmother who had put him on the throne, eventually realized that Elagabalus was a political liability. To save the Severan dynasty, she persuaded him to adopt his younger cousin, Alexander Severus, as his heir. When Elagabalus realized his cousin was more popular than he was, he tried to have the boy assassinated, but the plan backfired.

In March 222 AD, the Praetorian Guard, fed up with the Emperor's antics and encouraged by Julia Maesa's gold, mutinied. They found Elagabalus and his mother, Julia Soaemias, hiding in a latrine and killed them both. Their bodies were dragged through the streets and thrown into the Tiber River. The Senate immediately ordered the black stone to be sent back to Syria and the name of Elagabalus to be erased from all public records, welcoming the calm, obedient Alexander as his successor.
