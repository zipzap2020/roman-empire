Maximian

Maximian: The Herculean Hammer

Marcus Aurelius Valerius Maximianus, known as Maximian, was the iron fist to Diocletian’s brilliant mind. While Diocletian was the philosopher king who redesigned the Roman state, Maximian was the loyal soldier who spent nearly two decades on the front lines making those designs a reality. Elevated to the rank of Augustus in 286 AD, he took responsibility for the western half of the empire. He styled himself as the earthly representative of Hercules, the mythological hero of legendary strength and labor, while his partner Diocletian took the mantle of Jupiter.

The Defender of the West

Maximian’s reign was a marathon of warfare. He spent years campaigning against Germanic tribes like the Alamanni and Burgundians on the Rhine, and crushing the Bagaudae—peasant rebels who had turned to banditry in the war torn Gallic countryside.

His most frustrating challenge was the revolt of Carausius, a Roman naval commander who declared himself emperor of Britain and parts of northern Gaul. While Maximian’s initial attempts to reclaim Britain failed due to poor weather and naval setbacks, he successfully stabilized the mainland, allowing his junior Caesar, Constantius Chlorus, to eventually finish the job.

The Reluctant Abdication

In 305 AD, at the height of their power, Diocletian enacted his plan for a peaceful succession. He forced a reluctant Maximian to retire alongside him. While Diocletian happily went off to garden in Dalmatia, Maximian hated the quiet life. He had spent his entire adulthood in command, and the transition to a private citizen in southern Italy felt like a prison sentence.

It didn't take long for him to find a way back into the fray. When his son Maxentius revolted in Rome in 306 AD, Maximian eagerly came out of retirement to support him, reclaiming his title as Augustus and helping to defeat the official emperors sent to stop them.

The Final Betrayal

Maximian’s second act was defined by betrayal and family drama. He eventually quarreled with his son and fled to the court of his son in law, Constantine. Despite being treated with honor, the old soldier couldn't stop plotting. While Constantine was away fighting on the Rhine, Maximian seized the treasury at Arles and attempted to declare himself emperor for a third time.

The rebellion failed almost immediately. Constantine returned with a speed that trapped Maximian in Marseille. In 310 AD, the man who had helped save the empire from collapse was given a choice: take his own life or face execution. He chose the former, hanging himself in his quarters.

A Complicated Memory

Maximian was a man of the old world—a brutal, effective general who didn't understand the new, subtle politics of the Tetrarchy he helped build. Though his end was ignominious, his decades of service provided the military shield that allowed Diocletian's administrative reforms to take root. Without Maximian’s tireless campaigning, the Western Empire likely would have collapsed decades earlier.
