Gordian II: The Short-Lived Co-Emperor

Marcus Antonius Gordianus Sempronianus Romanus Africanus, known to history as Gordian II, served as co-emperor with his father for just over three weeks in the chaotic year of 238 AD. While his father provided the gray haired gravitas and senatorial prestige, the younger Gordian was meant to be the man of action. A high living aristocrat who was said to possess a library of 62,000 books and a social circle just as vast, he found himself suddenly thrust into a military role he was entirely unprepared for.

Life Before the Purple

Before his brief rise to the throne, Gordian II had a typical, successful career for a man of his high birth. He had served as consul and accompanied his father to the province of Africa as a legal advisor. Historians of the time described him as a man of immense charm and appetite, famously rumored to have had twenty two concubines and a love for the finer things in life.

When the tax revolt in Thysdrus forced his father into the role of emperor, Gordian II was the natural choice for co-ruler. The Senate in Rome, desperate for an alternative to the brutal Maximinus Thrax, confirmed both men immediately. They were seen as a return to the cultured, "civilized" emperors of the past.

The Clash Outside Carthage

The dream of a Gordian dynasty met a cold reality in the form of Capelianus, the governor of neighboring Numidia. Capelianus had an old personal grudge against the Gordians, but more importantly, he had the Legio III Augusta—the only professional military force in the region.

When Capelianus marched on Carthage, Gordian II did not hide behind the city walls. He gathered a massive force of local citizens and volunteers. While they were numerous, they were essentially a civilian mob armed with hunting tools and household blades. They stood no chance against the heavy infantry and disciplined cavalry of a Roman legion.

A Tragic End

The battle took place during a fierce storm. The professional legionaries easily broke the lines of the Carthaginian militia. In the slaughter that followed, Gordian II was killed. His body was so badly mangled in the mud and the crush of the retreating crowd that it was never recovered for a proper burial.

His death triggered his father’s suicide and left the Senate in Rome in a state of panic, as Maximinus Thrax was still marching toward Italy. However, the Gordian name did not die with him. His sister’s son—Gordian III—would soon be elevated by the people of Rome, carrying the legacy of his uncle and grandfather back into the imperial palace.
