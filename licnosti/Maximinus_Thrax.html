Maximinus Thrax

Maximinus Thrax: The Giant of the Frontiers

Gaius Iulius Verus Maximinus, better known as Maximinus Thrax, was the man who smashed the last remnants of the Severan dynasty and dragged Rome into the "Crisis of the Third Century." He was the first of the so called Barracks Emperors—men who rose from the lowest ranks of the army to the highest office through sheer force and the loyalty of their troops. Born in Thrace to a family of low social standing, he was a massive man, reportedly over eight feet tall, who personified the raw military power that would dominate Roman politics for the next fifty years.

From Peasant to Princeps

Maximinus’s rise was a military fairy tale. He joined the army during the reign of Septimius Severus and quickly became famous for his incredible strength and size. Ancient accounts claim he could pull fully loaded wagons by himself and crumble stones in his hands. He was the ultimate "soldier’s soldier," a man who spoke Latin with a thick accent and had no interest in the sophisticated culture of the Roman Senate.

When the legions grew tired of Alexander Severus’s diplomatic approach to the Germanic tribes, they looked to Maximinus. In 235 AD, he was proclaimed emperor by his troops. Unlike every emperor before him, Maximinus never bothered to visit Rome to confirm his title with the Senate. He spent his entire three year reign on the frontiers, living in a tent and leading his men from the front.

War on All Fronts

Maximinus was an effective general. He crossed the Rhine into Germany, deep into territory the Romans had avoided for decades, and devastated the Germanic tribes in their own heartlands. To pay for these massive military campaigns, however, he had to resort to extreme measures.

He confiscated the wealth of the aristocracy and even stripped the gold and silver from public temples. This won him no friends in the capital. To the Roman Senate, he was not a protector, but a barbarian tyrant who was bankrupting the empire to feed his soldiers. His reign became a cycle of warfare abroad and growing resentment at home.

The Rebellion of 238 and the Siege of Aquileia

The breaking point came in 238 AD, a year that would see no fewer than six different men claim the title of Emperor. A revolt in Africa sparked a massive movement against Maximinus, and the Senate officially declared him a public enemy. Enraged, Maximinus marched his seasoned Danubian legions toward Italy to reclaim his capital.

He reached the city of Aquileia, which stood as the gateway to Italy. To his surprise, the city closed its gates and refused to surrender. What followed was a long, grueling siege. As the soldiers began to starve and grow frustrated by the stubborn resistance of the townspeople, their loyalty to their "Giant" vanished.

In May 238 AD, soldiers from the Second Parthian Legion entered Maximinus’s tent at midday and murdered him along with his son. His head was cut off and sent to Rome on a pole. Maximinus Thrax had proved that the army could make an emperor, but he also proved that if an emperor couldn't keep his men fed and victorious, they would discard him just as quickly as they had crowned him.
