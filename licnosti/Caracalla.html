Caracalla

Caracalla: The Tyrant in the Soldier’s Cape

Born Lucius Septimius Bassianus, the man history remembers as Caracalla was one of the most ruthless and erratic emperors to ever wear the purple. He earned his nickname from the "caracalla," a hooded Gallic cloak he made fashionable among the legions. His reign from 198 to 217 AD (ruling alone after 211 AD) was defined by a total reliance on military force and a complete lack of the administrative subtleness his father had possessed. He is remembered for two things above all: the murder of his own brother and a law that changed the meaning of being "Roman" forever.

A Throne Soaked in Fratricide

Septimius Severus had hoped his two sons, Caracalla and Geta, would rule together in harmony. Instead, they shared a hatred so deep they divided the imperial palace into two armed camps. In December 211 AD, only months after their father's death, Caracalla arranged a "reconciliation" meeting in their mother’s apartments. As soon as Geta entered, Caracalla’s centurions rushed in and stabbed him to death while he clung to his mother, Julia Domna.

Following the murder, Caracalla launched a "Damnatio Memoriae" of unprecedented scale. He executed an estimated 20,000 of Geta’s supporters and ordered his brother’s face chiseled off every coin, statue, and monument in the empire.

The Constitutio Antoniniana

In 212 AD, Caracalla issued one of the most significant decrees in history. The Edict of Caracalla granted full Roman citizenship to nearly all free inhabitants of the Empire. While this sounds like a noble gesture of equality, contemporary historians like Cassius Dio argued it was purely a tax grab. By making everyone a citizen, Caracalla could collect inheritance and manumission taxes from a much larger pool of people to pay for his massive military spending.

The Baths and the Alexandrian Massacre

Caracalla left a permanent mark on the city of Rome with the construction of the Baths of Caracalla. This was not just a place to wash; it was a massive leisure complex covering 27 acres, complete with libraries, gyms, and gardens. It remains one of the greatest feats of Roman engineering, featuring a complex system of underground furnaces to heat the water and air.

His temper was legendary. When the citizens of Alexandria in Egypt mocked him with satirical plays about his brother's murder, Caracalla visited the city and ordered his troops to massacre the youth of the city and sack its treasures. He viewed himself as a new Alexander the Great, even organizing a "Macedonian Phalanx" of 16,000 men armed with traditional pikes.

Assassination on the Road

Like his father, Caracalla was constantly on the move with the army. In 217 AD, while traveling from Edessa to Carrhae in the East, he stopped by the side of the road to relieve himself. A disgruntled soldier named Martialis, who had been passed over for promotion, took the opportunity to stab the Emperor to death.

The plot was orchestrated by his Praetorian Prefect, Macrinus, who feared for his own life. Caracalla died at the age of 29, leaving behind an empire that was financially exhausted and a military that felt it could now make or break an emperor at will.
