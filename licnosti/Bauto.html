Bauto: The Frankish Protector of the Throne

Flavius Bauto was a powerful Frankish general who rose to the highest ranks of the Roman military during the late fourth century. Like many talented Germanic leaders of his time, he became a pillar of the Roman establishment, eventually serving as the Magister Militum, or Master of Soldiers, for the Western Empire. His career is a prime example of how the late Roman state relied on barbarian generals to maintain its borders and political stability.

A Loyal Guardian

Bauto is best remembered for his unwavering loyalty to the family of Emperor Valentinian I. When the usurper Magnus Maximus rose to power in Britain and Gaul, Bauto acted as the primary protector for the young Emperor Valentinian II in Italy. Under the direction of the Eastern Emperor Theodosius I, Bauto led a significant force of barbarian mercenaries into the Alps to block the passage of Maximus’s troops, effectively preventing a full scale invasion of Italy for several years.

Beyond his military duties, Bauto was deeply involved in the religious and political debates of the day. He was a pagan, yet he worked closely with the Christian court. He famously clashed with Ambrose, the influential Bishop of Milan, over the restoration of the Altar of Victory in the Roman Senate. Despite these religious differences, his military competence made him indispensable to the imperial family.

Death and Dynasty

Bauto died of natural causes around the year 388, shortly before the final conflict between Theodosius and Magnus Maximus. Although his life ended, his influence continued through his daughter, Aelia Eudoxia. She went on to marry the Eastern Roman Emperor Arcadius and became one of the most powerful and controversial empresses in Byzantine history.

Historians often note that Bauto was succeeded in his military influence by Arbogast, another Frankish general who was likely his kinsman. While Arbogast eventually took a more rebellious path, Bauto’s legacy remained that of a dedicated defender of the legitimate imperial line, proving that a leader of Frankish blood could be more Roman than many born within the empire's borders.
