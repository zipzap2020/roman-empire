Philip the Arab: The Millennium Emperor

Marcus Iulius Philippus, known as Philip the Arab, rose to power in 244 AD following the murky death of young Gordian III on the Persian frontier. Born in a small town in modern day Syria, Philip was the son of a local chieftain, making his ascent to the Roman throne a testament to how far the empire's social boundaries had stretched. While his reign began with a controversial peace deal with the Persians, he is most famous for hosting the grandest party in Roman history: the celebration of Rome’s 1,000th anniversary.

Peace at a High Price

Philip’s first task as emperor was to extract the Roman army from Persian territory. To do this, he negotiated a treaty with Shapur I that many Romans found humiliating. He agreed to pay a massive indemnity of 500,000 gold denarii and ceded influence over Armenia.

Despite the cost, the peace allowed Philip to return to Rome and secure his position with the Senate. He went to great lengths to show respect for the traditional institutions, promoting his son, Philip II, to the rank of Caesar to establish a new dynasty. He also founded a new city in his homeland, Philippopolis, which he filled with grand Roman architecture to honor his origins.

The Ludi Saeculares: Rome at 1,000

In April 248 AD, Philip presided over the Secular Games to celebrate the millennium of the founding of Rome. According to legend, Romulus had founded the city in 753 BC, and Philip spared no expense to mark the milestone.

The Colosseum was the stage for spectacular displays featuring exotic animals—including hippos, rhinos, and lions—and thousands of gladiators. For a few brief days, the internal rot and external threats of the 3rd century were forgotten as the citizens of Rome celebrated their city’s incredible longevity. Some later Christian writers even claimed Philip was the first Christian emperor because of his perceived leniency toward the faith, though most historians believe he remained a traditional pagan.

The Rebellion of Decius

The glitter of the millennium celebrations soon faded under the weight of mounting crises. Invasions by the Goths on the Danube and the rise of usurpers in the East drained the treasury and exhausted the army’s patience. Philip sent a respected senator named Decius to restore order to the Danubian legions.

Decius was too successful. The soldiers, impressed by his leadership and frustrated by Philip’s perceived weakness, proclaimed Decius emperor. The two forces met at the Battle of Verona in 249 AD. Philip was killed in the fighting, and his young son was murdered shortly after in Rome. Philip had presided over Rome’s greatest birthday, but he could not survive the deadly politics of the era he helped create.
