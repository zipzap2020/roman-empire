Maxentius

Maxentius: The Rebel of the Tiber

Marcus Aurelius Valerius Maxentius was the ultimate political outsider of the Tetrarchy. As the son of the retired Emperor Maximian, he felt entitled to power, yet he was pointedly passed over during the official promotions of 305 AD. While the official emperors were busy on the frontiers, Maxentius tapped into the growing resentment of the city of Rome, which had been demoted from a capital to a mere provincial city. In 306 AD, he seized the Eternal City and held it for six years, defying every "legitimate" army sent to remove him. Though history, written by his enemies, remembers him as a tyrant, his reign was a period of massive construction and a revival of Rome’s ancient prestige.

The Roman Resurrection

Maxentius was the last emperor to truly treat Rome as the center of the world. While other emperors were building palaces in Germany or Turkey, Maxentius poured his wealth into the city on the Tiber. He began the construction of the massive **Basilica of Maxentius** in the Roman Forum—the largest building ever constructed there—and built a sprawling imperial villa and circus on the Appian Way.

He marketed himself not as a cold bureaucrat, but as the *Conservator Urbis Suae* (Preserver of His Own City). He lowered taxes for the Roman citizens and won the loyalty of the Praetorian Guard, who saw him as their only champion in a world that had forgotten them.

Defying the Tetrarchs

Maxentius was remarkably successful at defending his stolen prize. He defeated the official Western Augustus, Severus II, by bribing his troops, and he later forced the senior Eastern Emperor, Galerius, into a humiliating retreat from the gates of Rome.

For a time, Italy and North Africa were his private domain. He even survived a coup attempt by his own father, Maximian, who tried to rip the purple robes off his back during a public assembly. By 312 AD, however, his luck had run out. His brother-in-law, Constantine, was marching from Gaul with a veteran army, determined to end the "tyranny" in Rome.

The Milvian Bridge

In October 312 AD, Maxentius made a fatal tactical error. Instead of staying behind the impregnable Aurelian Walls and letting Constantine starve, he chose to meet him in open battle at the **Milvian Bridge**.

During the chaotic retreat, the temporary bridge Maxentius had built collapsed. The Emperor, weighed down by his heavy armor, fell into the Tiber and drowned. His body was pulled from the mud the next day, and his head was cut off and paraded through the streets. Constantine would go on to claim that he won the battle because of a vision from the Christian God, while Maxentius was relegated to the role of the villainous pagan in the official imperial narrative.
