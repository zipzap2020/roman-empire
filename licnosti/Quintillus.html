Quintillus: The Shadow of a Brother

Marcus Aurelius Claudius Quintillus holds one of the most fleeting and debated spots on the list of Roman Emperors. The younger brother of the heroic Claudius Gothicus, he rose to power in 270 AD following his brother’s sudden death from the plague. His reign was a brief, desperate attempt to maintain a family dynasty in an age that only respected the sword. Depending on which ancient historian you believe, his time in the purple lasted anywhere from a few months to a mere seventeen days.

A Tale of Two Confirmations

When news of the death of Claudius Gothicus reached the army and the Senate, a divide immediately opened up. Quintillus was at Aquileia, guarding northern Italy. The Senate, eager to maintain some control over the imperial succession and favoring a man with a traditional aristocratic temperament, immediately proclaimed him emperor.

However, the tough Illyrian legions on the Danube frontier had a different idea. They had no interest in the Senate's preferences and threw their support behind their own commander, the legendary general Aurelian. Aurelian had been the right-hand man of the deceased Claudius and was seen by the soldiers as the true heir to his military legacy.

The Collapse of Authority

Quintillus found himself in an impossible position. He lacked the battle-hardened reputation of Aurelian and the massive military force required to back up his claim. As Aurelian began his march toward Italy, the troops stationed with Quintillus realized they were on the losing side. In the 3rd century, loyalty was a luxury that few soldiers could afford when faced with a superior enemy.

Deserted by his supporters and facing a certain defeat, Quintillus’s reign came to an abrupt and violent end. The exact circumstances are a mystery: some accounts say he was murdered by his own men, while others suggest he took his own life by opening his veins, choosing a dignified Roman death over the humiliation of execution.

The Briefest of Legacies

Despite the shortness of his reign, Quintillus managed to produce a surprising amount of coinage, suggesting he was very active in trying to secure his legitimacy during his few weeks of power. His death cleared the way for Aurelian to take the throne without a bloody civil war, allowing the new emperor to focus his energy on the monumental task of stitching the fractured empire back together.
