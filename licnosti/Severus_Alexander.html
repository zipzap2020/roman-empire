Severus Alexander

Alexander Severus: The Gentle End of a Dynasty

Marcus Aurelius Severus Alexander was the final emperor of the Severan dynasty. Taking the throne at just thirteen years old in 222 AD, he was the antithesis of his cousin Elagabalus. Where his predecessor was chaotic and provocative, Alexander was studious, respectful, and deeply devoted to the traditional Roman way of life. His thirteen year reign was a sincere attempt to return to the stability of the "Good Emperors," but he governed an empire that had become too militarized for a man of peace to handle.

The Rule of the Mothers

Because he was so young when he took power, the true authority rested with his grandmother, Julia Maesa, and his mother, Julia Mamaea. They established a council of sixteen distinguished senators to advise the boy, effectively restoring the prestige of the Senate that had been eroded since the time of Septimius Severus.

His mother, Julia Mamaea, was the dominant force in his life. She was a woman of immense intelligence and ambition who even invited the Christian theologian Origen to court to discuss philosophy. However, her tight control over her son and the imperial purse strings eventually earned her the resentment of the Roman soldiers, who viewed Alexander as a "mamma’s boy" who was too stingy with their pay.

Rising Threats in the East and West

The peace of Alexander’s reign was shattered by the rise of a new and much more dangerous power in the East: the Sassanid Empire. Led by Ardashir I, the Sassanids overthrew the Parthians and sought to reclaim all the lands once held by the ancient Persians, including Roman Syria. Alexander led a massive campaign against them in 232 AD. While he managed to halt their advance, the Roman losses were heavy, and the army was far from impressed with his leadership.

No sooner had he returned from the East than the Germanic tribes began crossing the Rhine and Danube frontiers. Alexander rushed to the north, but instead of seeking a decisive military victory, he attempted to buy peace through diplomacy and subsidies. To his battle-hardened legions, this was the ultimate betrayal. They felt their emperor was a coward who was paying the enemy with the gold that should have been their own.

The Bridge at Mogontiacum

The end came in 235 AD at a military camp near Mogontiacum (modern Mainz, Germany). The soldiers, led by a towering Thracian officer named Maximinus Thrax, mutinied. They burst into the imperial tent where Alexander was clinging to his mother, blaming her for the disastrous policies that had led them to this point.

Both Alexander and Julia Mamaea were murdered on the spot. With his death, the Severan line was extinguished. His murder triggered the "Crisis of the Third Century," a fifty year period of near-constant civil war, plague, and economic collapse that almost destroyed the Roman Empire. The age of the civilian-minded emperor was over; the age of the "Barracks Emperors" had begun.
