Lucius Cornelius Sulla

Sulla: The Dictator Who Rewrote the Republic

Lucius Cornelius Sulla Felix was a brilliant general and a ruthless politician whose actions changed the course of Roman history forever. A complex man of high culture and brutal efficiency, he was the first Roman general to use his own army to seize power in the capital. While he claimed his goal was to restore the traditional Roman Republic, his methods effectively proved that the old system could no longer survive the ambitions of its leading men.

The Rise and the Rivalry

Sulla first gained fame as the lieutenant of Gaius Marius during the Jugurthine War. It was Sulla who actually captured the enemy king, Jugurtha, a feat that sparked a lifelong jealousy between him and Marius. He further distinguished himself during the Social War, where his lightning fast victories against the rebelling Italian allies earned him the Grass Crown, the highest military honor in Rome, awarded by soldiers to a commander who saved an entire army.

The breaking point came when the Senate granted Sulla the command against King Mithridates of Pontus, only for the aging Marius to have the decision overturned through political maneuvering. In an unprecedented move, Sulla convinced his legions that the political move in Rome was a personal insult to them. He marched his six legions on Rome, forced Marius into exile, and reestablished his authority before heading east to fight the Mithridatic Wars.

Dictatorship and Proscriptions

After defeating Mithridates, Sulla returned to Italy to face the supporters of the now deceased Marius. Following a bloody civil war, he emerged victorious at the Battle of the Colline Gate and was named Dictator with no time limit on his power. To eliminate his enemies, he introduced the "proscriptions," public lists of individuals who were declared outlaws. Anyone on the list could be killed for a reward, and their property was seized by the state.

With his power absolute, Sulla enacted sweeping constitutional reforms. He stripped the Tribunes of the Plebs of their veto power, increased the size of the Senate, and reorganized the courts. His intention was to put the power back into the hands of the aristocracy and prevent future generals from following in his footsteps.

A Strange Retirement

In a move that shocked the Roman world, Sulla resigned his dictatorship in 79 BC and retired to his country villa. He spent his final months writing his memoirs and surrounding himself with actors and musicians. He died shortly after, and his massive funeral was a display of both the fear and respect he commanded. His epitaph, which he supposedly wrote himself, stated that no friend ever did him a kindness and no enemy a wrong without being repaid in full.
