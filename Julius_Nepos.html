Julius Nepos: The Last Legitimate Emperor of the West

Julius Nepos holds a unique and somewhat tragic place in the history of the Roman Empire. While many history books point to the young Romulus Augustulus as the final emperor, most historians consider Nepos to be the last ruler to hold the title with true legal authority. A native of Dalmatia and a member of a powerful military family, he was handpicked by the Eastern Roman Emperor Leo I to restore order to the crumbling Western provinces. He arrived in Italy in 474 with an army and the full backing of Constantinople, ready to reclaim the prestige of the Roman throne.

A Rule Between Italy and Dalmatia

During his brief time in power in Italy, Nepos proved to be an energetic leader. He focused his efforts on saving the remaining Roman territories in Gaul, which were being rapidly consumed by the Visigoths. He used a combination of military force and strategic diplomacy, even sending bishops as ambassadors to negotiate peace treaties. However, the Western Empire was so exhausted of resources that Nepos was forced to make difficult concessions, ceding land in Gaul to protect the Italian heartland. This made him unpopular with the local Roman aristocracy, who felt he was giving away too much of their heritage.

His reign in Italy came to an end in 475 when his own military commander, Orestes, turned against him. Orestes led a rebellion and marched on the capital of Ravenna. Lacking the troops to defend his position, Nepos was forced to flee by ship back to his home province of Dalmatia. Although Orestes placed his own son on the throne in Italy, the Eastern Empire continued to recognize Nepos as the only legitimate Western Emperor, a title he would hold in exile for the rest of his life.

The Final Years in the Palace of Diocletian

Nepos spent his final years ruling from the city of Salona and the grand Palace of Diocletian in what is now Split, Croatia. Even after the barbarian leader Odoacer took control of Italy and abolished the Western throne, he still formally acknowledged Nepos as the rightful emperor, even minting coins in his name. Nepos never gave up hope of returning to Rome and spent much of his exile petitioning the Eastern Emperor for military help to retake his lost capital.

His story ended violently in 480 when he was murdered by his own associates, possibly in the very palace where he sought refuge. With his death, the official line of Western Roman emperors finally went extinct. Julius Nepos remains a symbol of an empire that refused to die, a man who governed from the fringes of a world that was rapidly transforming into the kingdoms of the Middle Ages. His life connects the ancient history of Rome directly to the shores of the Adriatic, where his legacy still lingers in the ruins of Salona.
